What changed when we checked the whole record?

We counted the same three patterns in 21,306 NLP papers. All three rose above their earlier trend, and in ACL 2026 one opener pattern fell.

About 6 minutes · See all census figures

From a sample to a census

Our first study examined 9,472 computer-science papers and found three writing patterns that became more common around 2024. We then tested those patterns across the available main-conference record of ACL, EMNLP and NAACL, with the tests and thresholds recorded before extracting the census text.

21,368

Papers in the frame: ACL 2016–26, EMNLP 2016–25 and the NAACL years held in that period.

21,306

Papers analysed after the 1,500-body-word rule; 62 fell below it.

The census overlaps the original sample: 1,413 papers are shared. These are overlapping views of the record, so their counts must not be added. The excess remains when the known overlaps are removed, including a further sensitivity excluding 460 papers without a usable abstract for the overlap check.

The increase exceeds the earlier trend

We count functional -ing additions (“…, enabling faster training”), interpretive additions (“…, highlighting the importance of…”), and sentences starting with Additionally or Notably. Between 2022 and 2025, their rates rose roughly three- to fifteenfold, depending on venue and pattern.

A simple before/after comparison could mistake older drift for a new change. We instead extend each venue’s 2016–22 trend and compare it with 2024–25. The excesses below are equal-venue averages for ACL and EMNLP.

The adjustment makes these estimates smaller, by at most about 0.08 per 1,000 words. Most of the before/after difference remains above this fitted trend. This depends on the stated trend model; it does not identify a cause.

NAACL is not held annually, so it has a separate fit. It also sits above its earlier trend: +0.72, +0.19 and +0.36 for the three patterns.

Pretending the break happened earlier gives placebo excesses no larger than 0.05 per 1,000 words. Papers that our topic filter marks as not about language models still show increases, as do checks that remove Limitations sections or fit the trend only through 2021. These checks constrain simple explanations based on earlier drift or changing topics; they cannot exclude every alternative.

Papers by the same first authors changed

The conferences grew rapidly. Could the change simply reflect new people publishing? We followed 762 first authors who had papers in both 2020–22 and 2024–26. For these authors, the rise in their first-author papers was 58%, 67% and 72% of the rise across all papers, for the three patterns in the order above.

In the comparable pre-LLM comparison, 2016–18 to 2020–22, the three changes were at most 0.03 per 1,000 words. Holding first-author identity fixed therefore leaves much of the change visible. It does not establish who drafted or edited a paper, or measure how much change any particular person caused.

In 2026, one wording pattern fell

Before extracting ACL 2026 text, we registered a prediction that the combined Additionally/Notably rate would fall. It declined from 0.713 to 0.375 per 1,000 words, about 47%. The difference was −0.338, with a 95% interval from −0.369 to −0.306.

Almost all the combined decline came from Additionally; Notably held steady. Meanwhile, functional additions rose by another 0.233 per 1,000 words (95% interval 0.156 to 0.309). The patterns do not all move together. This does not tell us whether authors changed their habits, editing tools changed, or writing norms shifted.

What the checks can establish

A dependency parser classified 97–98% of the targeted -ing matches as attached clauses in both 2019–20 and 2024–25. Under a stricter definition restricted to adverbial clauses, the shares are 87–91%. Both definitions meet the equivalence rule set in advance; the interpretive-pattern check in 2019–20 uses only 190 matches. This is a parser diagnostic, not human validation of every match’s meaning.

Separate checks also find the three patterns rising in 1,192 analysed CVPR, ICML and NeurIPS papers (a 2025-minus-2020 comparison), 3,254 analysed USENIX papers out of a 3,258-paper frame (compared with trend), and 12,215 arXiv first versions out of 12,600 selected (also compared with trend). The arXiv sample excluded 376 papers below the word gate and did not retrieve nine. These comparisons use different groups of papers and different comparisons.

Two checks were weaker. In same-paper arXiv revisions, papers revised across late 2022 gained more functional additions and openers than papers revised across late 2020, but the paragraphs shared by both versions showed no clear difference. The data do not show authors rewriting existing text. A small biology sample did not meet the three-pattern rule.

The census primary estimates were independently recomputed from frozen numeric rows. In a separate review of secondary checks, seven interval endpoints differed enough to fail the original checking tolerance. Repeating the analysis with different random seeds gives results consistent with resampling noise, but does not establish exact agreement. The headline estimates do not change.

Study in progress

Next: outside computer science

We are preparing a test on arXiv mathematics, physics and quantitative biology. Metadata collection and statistical calibration precede the selection freeze and paper-text analysis. No outside-CS finding is available yet.

We will post results once they have been analysed and independently checked, reporting selected counts and analysed counts separately. Until results exist and have been checked, this section makes no claim about whether those fields changed.

A change in published prose, with its cause still open

The evidence supports a substantial change in these measured patterns, across venues, topic checks and repeat authors’ papers. It does not establish that AI caused it, estimate AI use in any individual paper, or justify judging an author from a phrase. Editing tools, imitation, changing topics and changing norms remain possible contributors.

Questions readers ask

Does finding one of these phrases identify AI use?

No. These are aggregate counts of wording patterns. A match in an individual paper does not establish how it was written and should not be used to accuse an author.

Could new authors explain the difference?

Changing authorship could contribute, but the first-author panel shows a change even when first-author identity is held fixed. It does not identify how much of the overall change was caused by a different mix of authors.

What does the parser check add?

It checks whether pattern matches are classified as attached clauses at similar rates in the two eras. It does not replace people checking whether each clause has the intended meaning.
