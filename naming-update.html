Research update · November 7, 2026

More papers name
their methods.

In our ACL sample, recent papers more often give their own method, framework, system, model or tool a name in the title or abstract. The difference is substantial. What caused it remains unresolved.

This asks a different question from our earlier work on writing style. We count names authors give to their own contribution, rather than mentions of existing products such as ChatGPT. Dataset and benchmark names are counted separately.

About three in ten more papers

An AI-assisted audit independently listed names in 100 papers before comparing them with an automatic extractor. Weighted to the sampled corpus, the estimated naming share is 27.0–30.6% in 2019–20 and 57.1–61.7% in 2024–26.

To bound the difference, we give ambiguous cases opposite answers in the two periods. The resulting endpoints are +26.5 and +34.7 percentage points. Sampling uncertainty is wider, as the second chart shows.

Dataset and benchmark naming is a separate result: an estimated increase of 10.2 percentage points, with an approximate interval from −4.4 to +24.7. This sample does not resolve its direction.

A difference is not an explanation

The audit compares two periods; it does not identify an effect of AI. A separate yearly series of automatically extracted candidates did not resolve a departure from the specified 2017–22 trend. That does not show the earlier trend explains the rise, or rule out a later change.

We also compared candidates within automatically assigned research areas. Much of the raw increase remains, but those topic labels are not validated well enough to establish a mechanism. In particular, the contribution labels miss too many resource papers to explain whether “Bench” names reflect naming habits or a changing mix of contributions.

A further descriptive check found 12 recent RAG names among papers marked neither agent-oriented nor LLM-centric. All 12 already carried a retrieval-topic label. This supplies no example of off-topic borrowing under those labels—and exposes a weakness in the LLM flag, rather than revealing authors’ motives.

We are preserving this as a descriptive result. The present evidence does not justify a new claim that AI caused the naming increase, or a paper explaining its mechanism.

What the estimate rests on

5,698ACL main-conference long papers in the two-period corpus
100probability-sampled papers: 20 from each of five years
Title + abstractthe same reading scope for the primary audit

The corpus contains 1,015 papers from 2019–20 and 4,683 from 2024–26. Sampling was stratified by year and extractor status, with 4–8 papers per stratum. Estimates use sampling weights; name-level calculations keep the paper as the sampling unit. Small strata make the intervals approximate.

The audit is an AI judgment reference, not a human gold standard. Independent computation checks reproduced the results. A targeted second reading covered all nine papers with unknown names and six other high-weight papers; it found no clear attribution conflict and changed no labels. That was neither blind relabeling nor a full second audit. Flipping one judgment on the highest-weight paper can move the recent-period estimate by about 4.4 percentage points.

The extractor’s recall may differ between periods. That limits comparisons made from its raw candidates; it is not a correction to the independently listed audit estimate. The audit also does not calibrate the unaudited intervening years.

Corpus boundaries and the yearly series

There are no Findings papers in these samples. Changes in the selection of main-track papers remain a possible explanation, not an established one. The yearly catalog differs from the audit’s corpus by seven papers in 2020, 2025 and 2026 combined. This mismatch concerns only the yearly series: the two-period raw comparison and topic adjustment use the same 5,698-paper corpus as the audit.
